People save travel videos constantly and then never do anything with
them. A restaurant gets named out loud in a thirty second clip, a
location tag flashes on screen, and the information sits there in a
format you can't search or plan against. Pinpoint pulls it out.

A user shares a TikTok or Instagram post from the iOS share sheet. The
backend creates a job, and a Python worker claims it, fetches the media
with yt-dlp or gallery-dl, and pulls audio through ffmpeg for
transcription. If the transcript comes back under fifty words, an OCR
gate fires and samples twenty evenly spaced frames through a vision
model. Transcript, caption, and any OCR text then feed a single
extraction call, and each candidate place carries a record of which
signal produced it. Google Places enriches each candidate, a
name-similarity step tags the match as exact, fuzzy, or weak, and
confidence scoring boosts candidates that several independent signals
agree on. Nothing becomes a pin without the user approving it from a
review screen that sorts candidates into Strong, Likely, and
Worth-a-look.

The failure mode that drove most of the engineering was bare names
resolving to the wrong city. A Chicago coffee video produced a confident
match on a restaurant in Memphis. A video mentioning "Bodega" matched a
Los Angeles importer on one run and a Charlotte deli on another, both at
maximum confidence, because the Places API will always find you
something. The fix was a geo-consistency pass that clusters a video's
resolved candidates and demotes geographic outliers, plus plumbing
TikTok's own location metadata through as a hint.

Then the fix broke on its own terms. A post titled "Matcha Spots in
Japan" is legitimately multi-city, and the distance check demoted all
five correct candidates. A related regression treated a prefecture-level
tag as a venue and collapsed an entire video into one pin labeled
"Kyoto." The resolution was label-first classification, separating
place-type tags from venue names before the geographic check runs, and
making the clustering aware that a video can have more than one valid
center. The general lesson is the one I'd lead with: a correctness check
tuned on single-location videos encodes an assumption about the content,
and the moment the assumption breaks it fails confidently in the other
direction.

The hardest decision with real alternatives was how to read on-screen
text. A local OCR engine had been built earlier and removed for being too
heavy. The options were re-adding it, calling a cloud vision API,
sampling at one frame per second, or sampling on scene changes. I chose a
gated call on a fixed twenty frames at low detail, because every other
option had unbounded cost on long videos while fixed sampling is
predictable, and low detail tested as effective as high. Gating on short
transcripts means the expensive path only runs when the cheap path has
already failed, which keeps it under a cent per triggered video.

Smaller things that cost real time: transcription mangled a venue name
into a phonetic near-miss, so the extraction prompt now prefers caption
spelling over audio when they disagree. A flat penalty on short "generic"
names was quietly suppressing legitimate Korean and Japanese venues,
since four characters means something different per script. And a patched
map library allocating roughly 176,000 style spans for a single long
route produced a 3.4 GB out-of-memory crash on device, which is now
capped.

Current state: submitted to the App Store as Pinpoint Travel Maps, with
TestFlight builds out, error monitoring, Sign in with Apple, and account
deletion in place. The core loop works reliably on tested posts.
Extraction quality across the long tail is not yet measured, since the
eval harness exists but no baseline run has been recorded, and the
scrapers depend on upstream tools that have broken twice in recent weeks,
which a daily canary now watches for. Real user numbers and a measured
accuracy baseline are the next things it needs.
